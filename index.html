CASE STUDY — EXTERNAL CFD

DrivAer vehicle aerodynamics: from wind tunnel to simulation

Full CFD simulation in Simcenter STAR-CCM+ of the standard DrivAer model (an automotive-industry benchmark geometry), validated against a reference thesis and Audi wind-tunnel data. 12M-cell mesh, RANS k-omega SST.

01 — Objective

Why this case

DrivAer is the standardized model the automotive industry uses to validate CFD methodologies before applying them to a real vehicle: published wind-tunnel data and reference studies exist to benchmark against. The goal was to reproduce the wake and the drag coefficient (CD) of the vehicle, following the methodology of a thesis from Universidad Politécnica de Madrid (OpenFOAM) but fully ported to Siemens STAR-CCM+, and to quantify the error between both approaches.

Vehicle configuration

  • DrivAer_E_S_woM_wW variant — Estate body, smooth underbody, no mirrors, detailed wheels
  • Longitudinal symmetry to reduce computational cost
  • Tire-to-ground contact patch explicitly modeled
  • Grille, engine, gearbox and exhaust excluded from scope
02 — Method

Geometry, domain and meshing

Geometry prepared in Siemens NX (native compatibility with STAR-CCM+) and simplified per the DrivAer standard. 25×10×8 m domain with progressive refinement toward the vehicle and its wake.

03 — Results

Convergence, CD and pressure distribution

Before trusting any result, mesh quality is validated: Wall y+ values must fall within the fully-turbulent region (30–600) across the whole surface.

04 — Wake

Vortex structure behind the vehicle

A vehicle's aerodynamic drag is largely decided in the wake: two counter-rotating vortices (upper and lower) form at the rear. Reproducing them with velocities comparable to the reference is the real test of the setup.

What I'd adjust on the next run

CASE STUDY — COMPOSITE STRUCTURES

Sizing a composite rear-wing spar for an FIA WEC Hypercar

Structural redesign of the internal spar element of a Le Mans Hypercar rear wing, replacing Aluminum 2024 with a Graphite/Epoxy laminate — sized against the FIA-WEC deflection regulation and cross-validated with FEA.

Key result
Composite spar deflection0.186 mm
FIA regulation limit10 mm
Vs. aluminum I-beam (1.35 mm)-86%
Compression margin of safety835%
Tension margin of safety38.8%
MaterialGraphite/Epoxy, 20 plies
01 — Objective

Why this case

Academic project for the Composite Materials course: a race team needs to cut structural weight without giving up stiffness. The target was the internal spar of a rear wing on a sport-prototype-class vehicle — replacing a conventional Aluminum 2024 section with a Graphite/Epoxy laminate, while staying inside the FIA-WEC deflection regulation and keeping (or improving) mechanical margins.

Reference vehicle: Toyota GR010 Hybrid (WEC Hypercar) Course: Composite Materials, UTN Haedo — 2023

Study scope

  • Full-vehicle CAD modeled from scratch in SolidWorks, from photos and the WEC technical regulations
  • Rear wing: single profile, fixed at both ends, simply supported at the center — the standard mounting for this vehicle class
  • Load case derived from real telemetry, not an isolated-airfoil assumption
  • Aluminum 2024 baseline compared against a Graphite/Epoxy redesign, both checked analytically and with FEA
02 — Method

Vehicle, wing geometry and the FIA load case

The full car was modeled first — not just the wing in isolation — because a rear wing sits directly in the wash of the entire vehicle upstream of it. Starting from a clean-flow airfoil study (as in classical Glauert-method theory) would have thrown away exactly that interference.

// FIA-WEC Article 3.8.4 — rear wing deflection
max. deflection @ 100 N5 mm
max. deflection @ 200 N10 mm
load/deflection ratioconstant over full operating range
support configurationfixed at both ends, simply supported at center
03 — Load case

Finding the critical speed: telemetry + CFD

Since the wing's aerodynamic design is fixed for the whole season, the worst case is simply the vehicle's top speed. Monza was selected as the reference circuit — its long straights produce the championship's highest aerodynamic loads at the end of each straight.

04 — Structural model

Shear, moment and the critical deflection point

The fitted pressure function was integrated twice — once for shear, once for bending moment — along the half-span, with the two boundary conditions the real mounting imposes: zero displacement at the fixed end, zero slope at the simply-supported end.

Result

Solving the deflection equation and setting its derivative to zero locates the critical point at y = 0.325 m — about 35% along the half-span — which is where every candidate section below is checked against the regulation.

05 — Baseline: Aluminum 2024

Two standard profiles, sized to the same envelope

Working back from the 10 mm deflection limit (with a 1.2 safety factor) gives a minimum moment of inertia of 15.57 cm4. Two standard profiles that fit inside the Gottingen 195 airfoil's internal envelope were checked against it: an I-beam and a circular tube.

Baseline choice

Both profiles clear the 10 mm regulatory limit by a wide margin, but the I-beam carries more than double the compression margin of safety of the tube for a similar footprint — it becomes the baseline the composite redesign is measured against.

06 — Composite redesign

Same envelope, Graphite/Epoxy laminate

The I-beam's external dimensions (80 × 46 × 3.8 mm) were kept unchanged so the comparison against the aluminum baseline is direct — only the material changes. A 20-ply unidirectional Graphite/Epoxy laminate (0.2 mm/ply) was laid up per element (both flanges + web), and classical lamination theory was used to get the effective bending properties.

transverse modulus, E210.3 GPa

effective flexural modulus, E'y66.36 GPa
coupling factor, η = E'y / E'x0.931
total moment of inertia (Steiner)6.19 × 10⁻⁷ m⁴
07 — Validation

08 — Lessons learned

What the numbers actually say

01

Full-vehicle CFD wasn't optional

The rear wing sits fully inside the wash of the car ahead of it. A clean-flow, isolated-airfoil method would have missed that interference entirely — modeling the whole vehicle first was the only way to get a realistic load case.

02

Compression and tension margins tell different stories

835% compression margin vs. 38.8% tension margin on the same section — the real design headroom for further weight savings sits on the tension side, not the compression side.

03

Same envelope means room left on the table

Keeping the aluminum baseline's outer dimensions made the material comparison clean, but it also means the composite section is still oversized for its new load case — a follow-up pass resizing the geometry itself, not just swapping material, would cut weight further.

CASE STUDY — THEORETICAL AERODYNAMICS

Finite-wing lifting-line analysis of the Horten IV flying wing

Classical lifting-line theory (Fourier-series / Glauert method) applied to a flying-wing planform reconstructed from historical technical drawings — replicating a published spanwise lift distribution and induced-drag polar from primary documentation.

Key result
CL total (lifting line)1.16
CDi (analytical)0.0247
Aspect ratio, b²/Sw21.3
Span / wing area20 m / 18.8 m²
Reynolds number1.5 × 10⁶
SolutionFourier series, m = 300
01 — Objective

Why this case

Starting from historical technical documentation on the Horten IV — a tailless flying-wing glider whose spanwise lift distribution and drag figures were published decades ago — the exercise was to rebuild the planform from scratch, solve it analytically with classical lifting-line theory, and check the result against the aircraft's own published curves. No CFD, no wind tunnel: just digitized drawings, 2D airfoil theory and a Fourier-series solution.

Course: Aerodinámica Teórica, TP N°4 — Ala Finita Institution: UTN Facultad Regional Haedo — 2025

Study scope

  • Full flying-wing planform reconstructed in CAD from historical wing-section drawings
  • Spanwise variation of airfoil shape, twist and elevon deflection captured station by station
  • Reference lift-distribution and drag-polar curves digitized from the aircraft's original documentation
  • Classical Prandtl lifting-line theory (Fourier series, m = 300 terms) solved symbolically in Mathematica
02 — Geometry

From historical drawings to a parametrized CAD wing

The only geometric source available was a set of scanned wing-section drawings from the aircraft's technical documentation. Each section's chord, span position and incidence were read off the drawings and rebuilt as a full 3D CAD model, preserving dihedral, sweep and the built-in washout between root and tip.

03 — Stations & profiles

Four spanwise stations, digitized profile by profile

The half-span was split into four reference stations, each with its own airfoil contour and, on the outer three, its own elevon. Every profile was digitized point-by-point with WebPlotDigitizer to build a clean coordinate cloud for 2D analysis.

04 — Airfoil analysis

2D lift-curve slope and zero-lift angle in XFLR5

Each digitized profile was analyzed individually in XFLR5 at Re = 1.5 × 10⁶, sweeping α from −3.5° to 20° in 0.5° steps. The linear region of each CL(α) curve gives the 2D lift-curve slope a₀ and the zero-lift angle αzl needed by the lifting-line formulation.

Technical note

Stations 2 and 3 each carry an elevon — this tailless design's only pitch and roll control — so their deflected profiles had to be re-digitized and re-analyzed separately: Station 2 → Surface 1 (a₀ = 6.39, αzl = 0.7°), Station 2 → Surface 2 (a₀ = 6.87, αzl = 6.3°), Station 3 → Surface 3 (a₀ = 8.60, αzl = 4.2°).

05 — Spanwise functions

Chord, twist and 2D lift slope as functions of y

Chord c(y), 2D lift-curve slope a₀(y), zero-lift angle αzl(y) and geometric twist αg(y) were each defined as piecewise functions of span position, one segment per station — mirroring how the wing is actually built and controlled. The classical substitution y = (b/2)·cos(π − θ) then converts every function into the θ-domain the Fourier solution requires.

06 — Lift distribution

Solving CL·c(y) and matching the reference report

With every spanwise function assembled, the Fourier-series lifting-line solution (m = 300 terms) gives CL(θ)·c(y) directly, and integrating it across the span returns a total CL of 1.16 for the α = 1° design condition. The reference report's own resultant curve (drawn at an Oswald-type factor δ = 30%) provides the benchmark.

Technical note

The two curves track closely in shape but not exactly in level — classical lifting-line theory has no way to account for dihedral or sweep, both of which this planform has in abundance. Given that limitation, the agreement is close enough to validate the spanwise parametrization itself.

07 — Induced drag

CL vs. CDi and the Oswald factor

The same Fourier coefficients that define the lift distribution also define the induced-drag polar: CDi = CL²/(π·AR)·(1 + δ), with the Oswald-type factor δ obtained from the ratio of higher-order to first-order coefficients.

Result

At CL = 1.16 the analytical solution returns CDi = 0.0247. As with the lift distribution, the gap against the reference polar is attributed to the same missing dihedral and sweep effects — the theory consistently reads slightly more efficient than the real, non-planar wing.

08 — Spanwise distributions

Downwash, induced angle, drag and circulation along the span

The same Fourier series also gives closed-form spanwise distributions for downwash velocity ωi, induced angle of attack αi, sectional induced-drag coefficient Cdi and circulation Γ — each one showing the discontinuities introduced by the wing's four-station, three-elevon planform.

09 — Lessons learned

What the comparison against the reference report actually shows

01

Sweep and dihedral are the real gap

Classical lifting-line theory has no mechanism for either effect. On a flying wing that leans on both for pitch and lateral-directional stability, that's not a rounding error — it's the dominant source of deviation from the reference curves.

02

Digitized data compounds through the whole solution

Airfoil contours, reference lift and drag curves — every WebPlotDigitizer extraction injects a bit of noise, and because the Fourier solution uses all of them together, small reading errors on the source drawings ripple through every downstream result.

03

Piecewise functions mirror how the wing is actually built

Chord, twist and 2D lift slope all had to be modeled as station-wise piecewise functions rather than smooth curves — which is a more faithful model anyway, since the real wing is four discrete panels and three independently deflecting elevons, not a continuous mathematical surface.

CASE STUDY — TURBOMACHINERY DESIGN

Designing a centrifugal compressor for a turbocharged production engine

Full CFTurbo design of a turbocharger compressor sized to double a naturally-aspirated engine's power output — impeller, diffuser and volute — exported to SolidWorks as a manufacturable 3D model, with first-pass performance maps.

01 — Objective

Why this case

Academic project for the Propulsión course: design a centrifugal turbocharger compressor for a naturally-aspirated production engine, sized to roughly double its power output within the blade-tip-speed and diffuser Mach-number limits set by the assignment. Every stage — impeller, diffuser, volute — was designed in CFTurbo and carried through to a manufacturable SolidWorks model.

Course: Propulsión, TP N°7 — Diseño de Compresor Centrífugo Institution: UTN Facultad Regional Haedo — 2024

Study scope

  • Target: roughly double the baseline engine's power output within blade-tip-speed and diffuser Mach-number limits
  • Mass-flow and pressure-ratio design point derived from the reference engine's intake requirements
  • Full centrifugal stage designed in CFTurbo: impeller, meridional flow path, diffuser and volute
  • Geometry exported to SolidWorks for a manufacturable 3D model and drawing set
02 — Baseline & design point

From a production engine to a compressor design point

The reference engine is a Renault-Nissan F4R 738 (75 Hp, 1149 cc, 4-cylinder). To roughly double its output, intake pressure has to rise by about 50% — which, combined with the engine's air demand, sets the compressor's mass-flow and pressure-ratio design point directly.

03 — Sizing

Locating the machine on the Cordier diagram

Before drawing a single blade, the design point's specific speed places the machine on the Cordier diagram: nq = 51.6 falls squarely in the centrifugal, low-pressure band, confirming a centrifugal impeller is the right architecture — not just a reasonable one — for this duty.

04 — Impeller geometry

Meridional flow path in titanium

The impeller's meridional contour was built with CFTurbo's "mean-line" mode, which contracts the flow section gradually rather than in abrupt steps — the gentlest path from eye to tip for a wheel spinning at 150,000 rpm. Titanium (ρ = 4507 kg/m³) was chosen for its strength-to-weight ratio at the temperatures and stresses this stage sees, with an unshrouded impeller and 0.28 mm tip clearance at both inlet and outlet.

05 — Blade design

14 blades, sized for shockless inflow

14 blades were chosen deliberately conservatively: enough solidity to do the work, without adding the mass and complexity a higher count would bring for no clear performance return. Blade shape is a ruled 3D surface with tangential thickness (0.6 mm at the hub, 0.402 mm at the shroud, both edges), incidence set for shockless inflow at 100% of the design flow at both hub and shroud, and slip modeled with the Aungier/Wiesner correlation.

06 — Diffuser & volute

Bleeding velocity into pressure, then collecting the flow

A vaneless free-form diffuser, in the same titanium, picks up right where the impeller tip leaves off and diverges the flow to bleed kinetic energy into static pressure. A single-type volute downstream is sized directly from the compressor's mass flow; at its inlet the flow is still at Mach 0.614 — comfortably subsonic and inside the assignment's Mach-number limit for the diffuser entry.

07 — Performance maps

Pressure ratio, power and efficiency vs. mass flow

With the full stage defined, CFTurbo's empirical correlations generate the compressor's characteristic curves. The software flags every one of them as a first-pass estimate: real performance would need experimental or CFD calibration to match reliably.

Technical note

Peak efficiency lands close to 88% near the design flow ratio, with the characteristic curves showing the expected shapes — but CFTurbo's own warning is worth repeating: these are empirical estimations, not a substitute for a rig test or a CFD run.

08 — Final model

From CFTurbo geometry to a manufacturable part

The finished stage geometry was exported and rebuilt in SolidWorks, turning the CFTurbo flow-path definition into a model ready for 2D manufacturing drawings — the deliverable the assignment actually asked for.

09 — Lessons learned

What sizing a compressor from scratch actually teaches

01

Specific speed picks the machine, not the other way around

Landing at nq = 51.6 on the Cordier diagram's centrifugal, low-pressure band confirmed the architecture before a single blade was drawn — skipping that check risks sizing the wrong type of machine entirely and finding out much later.

02

Blade count is a mass/complexity trade, not a performance dial

14 blades were a deliberate compromise: more would add solidity, but also weight and manufacturing cost with no guaranteed return for this duty cycle.

03

Empirical performance maps are a starting point, not a verified result

CFTurbo's own warning on every chart is the honest takeaway — pressure ratio, power and efficiency curves need experimental or CFD calibration before they can be trusted for the real engine.

01 — Objective

Same chassis, three fascias — does it matter aerodynamically?

Independent aerodynamics report on the Top Race V6 touring-car category, where every competitor races on one homologated spec chassis and body, differentiated only by a manufacturer-licensed front fascia — Ford, Lexus or FIAT. The study runs full external CFD on all three to answer a simple question for the series: how much does the fascia choice actually change drag, downforce and aerodynamic efficiency at race speed.

One spec chassis, three homologated noses

The TR V6 is a 3.5-litre naturally-aspirated V6 producing 390 hp, rear-wheel drive through a 6-speed sequential gearbox. Every car on the grid runs the same carrocería — body, splitter, floor, diffuser and rear wing are fixed by the category's technical regulations, with no modification permitted. The only visual and aerodynamic distinction between entries is the manufacturer-branded front fascia, sold as Ford, Lexus or FIAT dress panels over the identical structure underneath.

All three models land within 3% of each other on total drag and 9.5% on total downforce — the shared body dominates over fascia choice. Ford carries the most downforce and the most drag, but comes out as the most aerodynamically efficient of the three; Lexus is the cleanest but also the least efficient.

05 — Surface flow

Shear stress and pressure, side by side

Wall shear stress traces the surface streamlines directly onto the body, showing where the flow stays attached and where it separates; the pressure coefficient map shows the front-end stagnation and suction regions each fascia generates. Both are shown here in frontal view for all three models under identical color scales.

06 — Underbody flow

Streamlines through the splitter and floor

Underbody streamlines show the flow accelerating in under the splitter, staying attached across the flat floor, and feeding the diffuser — the single largest downforce contributor on all three models, ahead of the rear wing and splitter individually.

07 — Rear wing & wake

A short, clean wake — good for lap time, bad for slipstreaming

Rear-wing loading is close across all three cars (a 6% spread between Lexus, the highest, and FIAT, the lowest), though the pressure distribution along the span isn't perfectly uniform — an artifact of the wing-mount posts rather than the aerofoil itself, and a candidate for a cleaner support design. Downstream, the wake is short: turbulent "dirty air" extends only about 3 m behind the car before the flow settles, with a visible downwash following the tail shape.

Technical note

A short wake is exactly what a designer chasing low drag and high downforce wants — but it also means these cars generate little usable slipstream for a following competitor, unlike open-wheel cars that depend heavily on aerodynamic draft. It's a trade-off the report flags as worth revisiting from a racing-spectacle standpoint, separate from pure lap-time optimization.

08 — Lessons learned

What a three-way comparative CFD campaign actually shows

01

Shared geometry dominates over brand-specific styling

With drag within 3% and downforce within 9.5% across all three fascias, the spec body, splitter, floor and rear wing account for most of the aerodynamic performance — the fascia is a smaller lever than it looks from outside the car.

02

Highest drag and highest efficiency can coexist

Ford has both the most downforce and the most drag of the three, yet still posts the best aerodynamic efficiency — a reminder that ranking cars by a single coefficient in isolation misses how the two trade off against each other.

03

A clean wake is a double-edged result

The short, low-turbulence wake that comes with good drag and downforce numbers also limits how much slipstream a chasing car gets — a case where the aerodynamically "best" answer isn't automatically the best answer for racing on track.

CASE STUDY — RACE-CAR AERODYNAMICS

How rake angle reshapes a Formula 1 car's aero balance

High-fidelity CFD of a Formula 1 model at 1.0° and 3.0° rake — the "high-rake / double-setup" concept — quantifying how downforce distribution, drag and centre of pressure respond when only the car's pitch changes. CFD simulations run for a published technical article.

Key result — 3.0° vs 1.0° rake
Front-wing downforce+39.3 %

Total downforce−1.7 %

01 — Objective

Why this case

A high-rake car effectively runs two aerodynamic setups: low rake on the straights, where downforce compresses the rear suspension, and high rake under braking and through slow corners, where the rear rises and the front wing drops toward the track. The question for CFD was simple to state: with exactly the same car and no other changes, what happens to downforce, drag, efficiency and balance when the rake goes from 1.0° to 3.0°?

Role: CFD simulation & post-processing Output: Published technical article (English edition)

Study scope

  • One baseline F1 model, only the rake angle varied (1.0° vs 3.0°)
  • Two fixed, steady states — no transient pitch motion
  • No aero re-optimization for the high-rake setup: the response of the same geometry is what's being measured
  • Outputs: total downforce and drag, efficiency, front/rear balance, centre of pressure, underfloor pressure, streamlines
02 — Method

Two setups, one car

Changing the rake from 1.0° to 3.0° drops the front splitter from 12.5 mm to just 3 mm above the ground and raises the rear of the car — exactly what happens to a high-rake car on corner entry.

03 — Floor physics check

The "crack pressure" at the floor–diffuser junction

Before comparing setups, the expected underfloor behaviour was checked on a Formula-E-type model (250 km/h, 1° rake, rotating wheels): air accelerates at the floor entry, a sharp pressure drop forms right at the floor–diffuser transition, and the diffuser then recovers pressure back toward ambient.

04 — Front wing & balance

Ground effect moves forward

At 3.0° the front wing sits much closer to the track and its underside low-pressure regions grow substantially — together with the underside of the nose, the whole front assembly starts to behave like a front diffuser.

05 — Underfloor pressure

What the diffuser loses at high rake

Pressure was extracted along three longitudinal sections under the floor. At 1.0° there is a deep crack-pressure peak at the floor–diffuser transition; at 3.0°, with the rear ride height around 165 mm, that peak is much weaker and the floor–diffuser assembly produces less downforce.

08 — Lessons learned

What the numbers actually say

01

Distribution, not total

Total downforce barely moves (−1.7 %), but the balance shifts almost 10 points forward — which is exactly what reduces understeer on slow-corner entry.

02

The cost is drag

+15.8 % drag drops efficiency from 2.5 to 2.1. High rake pays off under braking and in slow corners, not on the straights — hence the need for the car to flatten out with speed.

03

The diffuser needs ride height control

With the rear at ~165 mm the crack-pressure peak collapses. The floor–diffuser assembly is the most sensitive element to the setup change.

01 — Objective

Why directional coefficients

Low pressure on a surface does not automatically mean downforce, and high pressure does not automatically mean drag — it depends on which way the surface faces. Projecting the local pressure contribution onto the longitudinal axis (Cpx: the more positive, the more drag) and the vertical axis (Cpz: negative = downforce, positive = lift) shows directly which zones of each part are doing useful work and which are costing drag. The goal was not to optimize the whole car at once, but to rank the candidate zones part by part.

02 — The model

Full-car F1 geometry

03 — Front wing

The undersides do the work — and pay the drag

On the top surfaces the third flap is the main drag source; the rest contribute far less. The less intuitive result is underneath: the flap undersides produce a lot of drag — and the underside of the wing is also where almost all of its downforce comes from, while the top surfaces only add a mild contribution.

04 — Rear wing

Simpler part, same counter-intuitive answer

Drag is concentrated strictly on the upper element, on both faces; the endplates contribute almost nothing. For downforce, the top surface — where the airflow visibly impacts — contributes much less than the underside, with the main element's underside producing the most load.

05 — Floor & diffuser

Ground-effect downforce, almost for free

The only real drag source on the underside is the leading section of the diffuser; the flat floor and the rear of the diffuser show essentially zero drag. Downforce comes from the front of the floor and plank (under the sidepods) and from the diffuser entry. A red patch just ahead of the rear tyres shows lift: the tyres act as a blockage, raising the local pressure on that part of the floor.

06 — Wheels & brake ducts

Tyres, rims and front brake cooling

As expected, the front faces of the tyres dominate their drag; the rear faces contribute much less — and the rear face of the front tyres less than that of the rears. Downforce on the tyres is limited to small areas near the contact patches. Rims and front brake ducts add drag, with small downforce zones on their upper surfaces.

07 — Bodywork & helmet

From the whole body down to the driver's helmet

On the bodywork the maps immediately flag the drag-generating regions worth working on, and show that some zones produce downforce and drag at the same time — including a downforce region just inside the sidepod inlets. The same post-processing scales down to small parts: on the helmet only a small frontal area produces drag, while other zones are marginally pulled forward.

08 — Lessons learned

What the maps actually say

01

Pressure is not force

A static-pressure plot alone is misleading; projecting it onto the axis of interest is what tells you whether a zone helps or hurts.

02

Look underneath first

Front wing, rear wing and floor all make most of their downforce on their undersides — that's where geometry changes pay off the most.

03

Next step: local efficiency

Beyond locating drag and downforce separately, the same fields can map local downforce-to-drag efficiency, ranking zones by how well they trade one for the other.
